How the System Works

Who actually pays
to fix a road?

Most people assume road money is local — that the township or county writes the check. It mostly isn't, and they mostly don't. Here's where the money really comes from, how it moves, and why a corridor study is the key that unlocks it for SR 28.

The One Idea That Explains Everything

Road money flows downhill — local governments apply for it.

Every time you buy gas, you pay a federal fuel tax and a state fuel tax. That money flows into big federal and state programs, and from there it flows back down to local road projects. The catch: it doesn't flow automatically. Somebody local — a county, a township, a transportation district — has to apply for it, project by project.

A corridor doesn't get improved because it needs it. It gets improved because someone asked — with the right paperwork, through the right agency, at the right time. That's not cynicism; it's just how the pipeline works. And it's why an organized, informed community matters.
Follow the Dollar

Three streams of money reach local roads.

Federal

The biggest stream

Federal gas tax dollars go to the Highway Trust Fund, then come back to each region through programs with names like STBG and CMAQ.

For our region, a planning agency in Cincinnati called OKI decides which local projects receive them — typically $40–80 million per year, awarded to counties, townships, and cities that apply.

State

Ohio's fuel tax

Ohio's motor fuel tax funds ODOT, which runs its own competitive programs — including one just for county transportation improvement districts and another (TRAC) for major projects like road widenings.

The Ohio Public Works Commission also helps fund local road work — Goshen Township recently received an OPWC grant to repave three township roads.

Local

The smallest slice — by design

Local governments rarely fund big road projects outright. Their main job is the local match — typically 10–20% of a project's cost — plus the application itself.

For a corridor study, that local share is modest, and it can be split among multiple partners: two townships, the county engineer, and the county's transportation district.

The Players

Three agencies you've probably never heard of decide what gets built.

None of them are secret — they're just invisible unless you go looking. Here's who they are in plain English.

OKI Regional Council of Governments

The gatekeeper for federal money

OKI is the federally designated planning agency for the eight-county Cincinnati region, including Clermont County. No federal transportation dollar is spent here without going through OKI's process. Local governments apply to OKI each year, and its board — which includes county and township officials — picks the winners.

OKI also runs corridor studies itself, as part of its annual work program. Studies exactly like the one SR 28 needs have been done this way in other parts of the region — mostly paid for with federal planning funds, with local partners covering a small share.

CCTID — Clermont County Transportation Improvement District

The county's project vehicle

The CCTID exists to line up funding for Clermont County road projects — pooling local money to generate the match that unlocks federal and state dollars. It has delivered more than 60 projects since 2006, and it commissioned the original 2013 SR 28 corridor study.

It's also the only local body eligible for ODOT's annual TID grant program, which awards up to $500,000 per project statewide — a natural fit for funding study and engineering work.

ODOT — the Ohio Department of Transportation

The state pipeline

ODOT District 8 manages the state highway system in our corner of Ohio — SR 28 is a state route, so major improvements to it ultimately run through ODOT's project pipeline, called the STIP. Getting a project into that pipeline is how it moves from idea to funded construction.

And the entry ticket to the pipeline is a current technical study that defines what's needed and what it costs.

Why the Study Comes First

The pipeline every funded road project follows.

Corridor study → Project enters state & regional plans → Funding awarded → Construction
  • Funding agencies don't award money to problems — they award it to defined projects with scopes, costs, and technical justification. A study is what turns "SR 28 is overwhelmed" into a fundable project.
  • The 2013 study briefly put SR 28 on this path — but its recommendations were never carried into the funding pipeline, and today it's too old to base decisions on.
  • A new study would assess the corridor as it actually is now — with all the development approved since — and give every agency above a current basis to act.
  • This is why CART's ask is a study rather than a demand to "just widen the road." The study is the step that makes everything after it possible.
Where You Come In

The system responds to organized communities.

Applications get filed when local officials believe residents care. Study requests get prioritized when agencies hear from the communities affected. That's not a flaw in the system — it's how the system is designed to allocate limited money. The corridors that get fixed are the ones whose communities showed up.

CART's job is to make sure SR 28 is one of them: documenting the corridor's condition, keeping the issue in front of local officials and funding agencies, and making it easy for residents to be counted.
